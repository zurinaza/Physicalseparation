Section 01
◆ Foundation

Unit operations

A unit operation is the place in a plant where the process actually happens. Separation processes are one family of them.

1.1 What a unit operation is

Definition
  • A basic step in a chemical process.
  • It involves a physical change or chemical transformation — separation, crystallisation, evaporation, filtration, polymerisation, isomerisation.
  • A process may need many unit operations to turn feedstock into product.
Why it matters
  • The main principles shared by all chemical industries.
  • The foundation for designing plants, factories and equipment.
🥛 Milk processing: homogenisation, pasteurisation and packaging are each a unit operation. Connected together, they make the overall process.

1.2 Plant anatomy: where unit operations sit

Upstream processing is where reactions occur. Downstream processing is where purification and separation occur. This course is about the downstream side.

1.3 Designing a unit operation

Step 1

Balances

Write a balance for each transported quantity of each component, as equations.

Step 2

Solve

Solve the equations for the design parameters of the process.

Step 3

Optimise

Select the optimum design.

Step 4

Equipment

Design the physical equipment — height, diameter and so on.

1.4 Five classes of unit operation

🌊 Fluid flow

Fluid transportation, filtration, solid fluidisation.

🔥 Heat transfer

Evaporation, drying, heat exchange.

🔁 Mass transfer

Gas absorption, distillation, extraction, adsorption.

❄️ Thermodynamic

Gas liquefaction, refrigeration.

⚙️ Mechanical

Solid transportation, crushing, pulverisation, screening, sieving.

This course

Mostly mass transfer (leaching, extraction) and heat transfer (drying, evaporation), with crystallisation combining both.

1.5 The focus of this course

Course focus

Understand the mechanism

Connect to design

Section 04
◆ Learning outcomes 1 & 2

The interacting systems in detail

Each system is governed by its own equilibrium. That equilibrium is the key to designing the operation.

4.1 Solid–liquid separation

Equilibrium separation when a solid phase (sediment or crystal) interacts with a liquid. Solid–liquid equilibrium (SLE) thermodynamics governs it.

Thermal / phase transition

Crystallisation

Leaves pure crystals from a concentrated solution. → Topic 5

Physical–mechanical

Sedimentation, centrifugation

Solids settle or are spun out of the liquid.

Extraction

Leaching

Extracting a soluble component from a solid using a liquid solvent. → Topic 1

☕ Making tea or coffee is leaching. Leaching is the first unit operation in this course.

4.2 Vapour–liquid separation

Depends on the thermodynamic equilibrium between the vapour phase and the liquid phase of a mixture. Vapour–liquid equilibrium (VLE) is mandatory for the design of these operations.

Distillation

By volatility

Separates solvents or hydrocarbons by volatility, e.g. acetone and water. The mixture is boiled; the more volatile component is condensed and collected.

Evaporation

Removes solvent as vapour to concentrate a solution. → Topic 4

4.3 Liquid–liquid separation

Focuses on the equilibrium between two usually immiscible liquids. Liquid–liquid equilibrium (LLE) is the design key.

Application 1

Solvent (liquid–liquid) extraction

A solute in one liquid is removed by contact with a second, relatively immiscible liquid solvent. → Topic 2

Application 2

Adsorption with a liquid adsorbent

A component is taken up from one liquid by an adsorbing liquid phase.

4.4 A real-world case: crude oil

Crude oil has many components with different boiling points. It is heated to vapour, and the vapour condenses at different heights of the column, where individual fractions are collected.

Section 01
◆ Learning outcome 1 & 5

What leaching actually is

A solvent walks into a solid, dissolves one component, and walks back out with it. That's the whole idea — everything after this is bookkeeping.

1.1 Definition and purpose

Purpose
  • Recover a desired solute from a solid matrix, or
  • Remove an undesirable solute from it,
  • using a liquid solvent and mass transfer.
Solid feed = insoluble carrier B + soluble compound A. Add solvent C, get two products out.
solute Ainert Bsolvent C

1.2 Leaching vs washing

Leaching

You want the dissolved component. Value leaves in the overflow. Example: hexane pulling oil out of soybean flakes.

Washing

You don't want it. An undesirable solute is stripped from the solid, usually with water. Value stays in the solid.

1.3 Two ways to classify a leaching plant

By time

Batch · semibatch · continuous

Batch: fill, soak, drain. Continuous: solids and solvent flow non-stop through the unit.

By direction

Crosscurrent · countercurrent

Crosscurrent gives fresh solvent to every stage. Countercurrent reuses it against the solid flow — far less solvent for the same recovery, so it dominates industry.

1.4 Where you'll meet it

🍵 Food & bio

Sugar from beet (water) · oil from soybean (hexane) · soluble tea from leaves · essential oils · ginger extract.

⛏ Metals

Copper from ore (sulfuric acid) · cobalt & nickel (acid–ammonia–oxygen) · gold (aqueous sodium cyanide).

💊 Pharma

Active compounds from stems, roots and leaves after drying breaks the cell wall.

☕ Your daily reactor: tea leaves = B, hot water = C, catechins & caffeine = A. The used bag you squeeze out is the underflow.

Resistance to diffusion inside the particle is normally far larger than resistance at the surface. That is why particle size is the design lever.

When surface transfer does control

Rate of dissolution of A into a solution of volume V:

2.4 Preparing the solid

Choice of pretreatment depends on how much solute there is, how it is distributed, whether it sits inside cells, and the original particle size.

Locked in a matrix

Solvent must diffuse in and out. Crush and grind to expose it.

Already in adhering solution

No grinding needed — simple washing recovers it.

Inside living cells

Cell wall and membrane block the solvent. Disrupt by homogenisation, osmotic pressure or ultrasound.

Sugar beet

Cut into thin wedges (cossettes) to shorten the water's diffusion path.

Pharma botanicals

Dry stems, roots and leaves first — drying ruptures the cell wall.

Soybean

Roll or flake to break cells and create thin sheets.

Section 03
◆ Learning outcome 3

Equilibrium, and the diagram you'll live on

Three symbols do all the work: N, xA, yA. Get these right and every stage calculation in sections 4 and 5 is just drawing lines.

3.1 Overflow and underflow

V · overflow

Clear liquid

Solvent C + dissolved A. No solid. Composition written as xA.

L · underflow

Wet slurry

Inert B + liquid of the same composition as the overflow. Composition written as yA.

In an ideal equilibrium stage: all solute dissolves, none of the carrier dissolves, and the liquid clinging to the solid matches the overflow, so yA = xA and the tie line is vertical. How much liquid clings depends on the equipment, not the chemistry.
⚠️ Careful: in leaching, x is the liquid overflow and y is the slurry liquid — the opposite convention to liquid–liquid extraction.

3.2 Defining N, xA and yA

Everything is expressed per kg of solution (A + C), which keeps the inert B out of the denominator.

N = kg B / (kg A + kg C)
xA = kg A / (kg A + kg C)  overflow
yA = kg A / (kg A + kg C)  underflow liquid
  • N = 0 for any overflow stream (no solid).
  • Dry feed with no solvent: yA = 1, N = kg B / kg A.
  • Pure fresh solvent: xA = 0, N = 0 — the origin.

Composition converter

Type any stream's masses. Watch where it lands on the diagram.

N = 0.667 · x/y = 0.167

3.3 Reading the equilibrium diagram

The upper curve is the underflow (N vs yA); the lower line on N = 0 is the overflow (N vs xA). Tie lines join streams that leave the same stage.

Section 05
◆ Learning outcome 3 & 4

Countercurrent multistage

Solids go one way, solvent the other. The exit solvent is richest where the solid is richest — which is why this layout beats crosscurrent on solvent economy.

5.1 The cascade and its numbering

Rule 1

Stages are numbered in the direction of the solid / underflow flow.

Rule 2

V = overflow (A + C). L = the solution retained by the solid, not the whole slurry.

Rule 3

B never dissolves, so the solid flow rate is constant across every stage.

5.2 Overall balances → point M

L₀ + VN+1 = LN + V₁ = M
L₀yA0 + VN+1xAN+1 = MxAM
B = N₀L₀ = NNLN = NMM
  • M sits where line L₀–VN+1 crosses line LN–V₁.
  • LN must lie on the underflow curve; V₁ must lie on N = 0.

5.3 The operating point Δ

Balance around the first n stages and rearrange — the difference between passing streams is constant everywhere in the cascade:

Δ = L₀ − V₁ = LN − VN+1
xAΔ = (L₀yA0 − V₁xA1) / (L₀ − V₁)
NΔ = B / (L₀ − V₁)

Because Δ is constant, every pair Ln / Vn+1 lies on a straight line through it. That single point turns the whole cascade into a fan of lines.

The stepping recipe

  1. Plot L₀, VN+1, LN; get M and V₁.
  2. Locate Δ = intersection of L₀V₁ and LNVN+1.
  3. Line Δ–L₀ hits N = 0 at V₁.
  4. Vertical tie line from V₁ up to the curve gives L₁.
  5. Line Δ–L₁ hits N = 0 at V₂. Repeat.
  6. Stop when you pass LN. Count the steps.

5.5 Variable vs constant underflow

Variable underflow

Viscosity and density change with solute concentration, so solids from the low-numbered (concentrated) stages retain more liquid. N vs yA is a curve; L and V vary stage to stage. Use the Δ-point construction above.

Constant underflow

Retention is the same at all concentrations, so N vs yA is a horizontal line and both L and V are constant. The operating line on an x–y plot becomes straight, so you can step off stages McCabe–Thiele style.

Stage 1 still needs separate treatment, because L₀ enters with little or no solvent and so L₀ ≠ LN.

The hardware

Every leaching plant does the same three jobs. The equipment differs only in how it moves solid and solvent past each other.

6.1 Three jobs, always

01

Dissolve

Bring solvent to the soluble constituent and get it into solution.

02

Separate

Split the solution from the insoluble residue.

03

Wash

Rinse the residue to free it of unwanted solute, or to claw back the last of the valuable one.

6.2 Equipment gallery

Six units you should be able to name, sketch and justify. Labels follow the standard nomenclature in Coulson & Richardson Vol. 2 and Geankoplis Ch. 12.8.

Fixed bed · batch

Batch percolation vessel

A vertical vessel split by a slanting partition. Seeds fill the upper section, fresh solvent is sprayed through a distributor, percolates down the bed, drains through the perforated floor into the lower compartment, and a steam coil strips solvent from the miscella.

Fixed bed · cellular feed

Sugar beet diffuser

Hot water enters the top of a closed vessel packed with beet cossettes; sugar solution leaves the bottom. The movable cover and bottom let spent slices drop out so a fresh charge can be loaded. Gentle percolation avoids breaking up the slices.

Fig. 6.2 — Typical fixed-bed apparatus for sugar beet leaching
Moving bed · bucket elevator

Bollman extractor

Perforated baskets circulate like a bucket elevator. Solid enters the top basket on the downward side and is discharged from the top basket on the upward side. Half miscella sprays the nearly spent solid (countercurrent); fresh solvent then runs co-currently down the remaining baskets.

Moving bed · screw conveyor

Hildebrandt extractor

Interconnected screw conveyors drag the solid up one leg while solvent flows down the other, giving true countercurrent contact in a compact U-shaped path. Dry feed enters one side; leached solid and oil-rich solvent leave the other.

What liquid–liquid extraction is

Also called solvent extraction. One or more solutes are removed from one liquid phase and transferred into a second, immiscible liquid phase.

1.1 Definition and principles

  • Normally one phase is organic, the other aqueous.
  • The solute moves from one liquid phase to the other by mass transfer.
  • At equilibrium the split is fixed by the distribution law.
  • The phases separate afterwards because they are immiscible.
  • Solute is then recovered from the extract — usually by distillation — and the solvent is regenerated.
Why bother when distillation exists? No vaporisation is needed, so it runs at low temperature. That saves heat-sensitive products, and it beats distillation on close-boiling mixtures and azeotropes.

1.2 The five steps of an extraction

Play it through, or scrub a step at a time. Every LLE unit — mixer-settler, column, centrifugal contactor — is doing exactly these five things.

Choosing a solvent

Ten criteria, two of which are numbers you can calculate. Get these wrong and no amount of clever stage design rescues the process.

2.1 Selectivity, β

The separation factor — how much better the solvent likes A than it likes the carrier B. Analogous to relative volatility in distillation.

β = (yA/yB) ÷ (xA/xB)
  • β > 1 is required, or no separation is possible at all.
  • The larger β is, the fewer stages you need.
  • β = 1 at the plait point, which is why you can never operate there.

Insolubility of the solvent

The less mutual solubility between carrier B and solvent C, the larger the two-phase region and the cleaner the split.

Not favourable — B and S dissolve in each other

Preferred — B and S have limited solubility

2.2 Distribution coefficient, K

K = (mass fraction A in extract) ÷ (mass fraction A in raffinate) = y/x

For acetone distributed between chloroform and water, K = 1.72 — acetone is preferentially soluble in the chloroform phase.

Large K is desirable: less solvent is needed for a given degree of extraction. But K alone is not enough — a solvent can have a high K and a useless β.

2.3 The other seven factors

Recoverability

No azeotrope with the solute, high relative volatility, small latent heat — you have to get the solute back out.

Density difference

Needed for the phases to settle at all. No Δρ, no separation.

Interfacial tension

High σ → easy coalescence but hard dispersion. Low σ → fine drops but emulsions. A genuine trade-off.

Chemical reactivity

The solvent must be stable and inert to feed, solute and materials of construction.

Viscosity

Low, for pumping and for mass transfer.

Vapour pressure

Low, to limit losses and handling hazard.

Flammability & toxicity

Non-toxic, high flash point. Often the deciding constraint in practice.

Cost

Cheap and available — you will be circulating a great deal of it.

2.4 Extraction modes

One stage is rarely enough. How you arrange the stages decides your solvent bill.

Crossflow

Fresh solvent to every stage. Recovers more than a single stage for the same total solvent, but produces a dilute combined extract and wastes solvent.

Countercurrent

Solvent meets the most exhausted raffinate last and the richest feed first. Less solvent, more concentrated extract — which is why almost every plant uses it.

Equilibrium diagrams

Three components, two phases. You cannot write a simple K and be done — you need a diagram, and you need to be able to read tie lines off it.

3.1 Phase rule

F = C − P + 2 = 3 − 2 + 2 = 3

Three degrees of freedom: temperature, pressure and one concentration. Fix T and P — as we always do — and one concentration fixes the whole two-phase system. That single remaining variable is what a tie line encodes.

Two systems you must recognise

  • Type I: only one pair partially miscible — e.g. MIBK(A)–water(B)–acetone(C). One envelope, one plait point.
  • Type II: two pairs partially miscible — e.g. styrene–ethylbenzene–diethylene glycol, or chlorobenzene–MEK–water. The envelope runs from side to side and there is no plait point.

3.2 The triangular diagram

How to read it

  • Each corner is a pure component.
  • The perpendicular distance from point M to a side gives the mass fraction of the component at the opposite corner.
  • xA + xB + xC = 1, always.
  • Inside the envelope: two phases. Outside: one phase.
  • A mixture at M splits into raffinate R and extract E at the ends of the tie line through M.
  • At the plait point P the two phases become identical, so R = E and β = 1.

4.2 The lever arm rule

Combining the overall and component balances:

L/V = (yA − xAM)/(xAM − xA)
L(kg)/V(kg) = length VM / length LM
L(kg)/M(kg) = length VM / length LV

So you can get the amounts with a ruler once you have the diagram. It is an estimate — always cross-check with the algebra.

Countercurrent multistage

Two entering streams, two leaving streams, one mixture point M and one operating point Δ. The construction is the same shape as the leaching one you already know — only the diagram underneath has changed.

5.1 The cascade

  • Feed L₀ enters stage 1; solvent VN+1 enters stage N.
  • Extract V and raffinate L flow countercurrently from stage to stage.
  • Raffinate composition is x, extract composition is y.

5.2 Overall balances → mixture point M

L₀ + VN+1 = LN + V₁ = M
xAM = (L₀xA0 + VN+1yAN+1)/(L₀ + VN+1)

Eq. 12.7-4

xCM = (L₀xC0 + VN+1yCN+1)/(L₀ + VN+1)

Eq. 12.7-3

  • L₀, VN+1 and M lie on one straight line.
  • LN, M and V₁ lie on another straight line.

5.3 Example 12.7-1 · overall material balance

Pure isopropyl ether at VN+1 = 600 kg/h extracts an aqueous solution of L₀ = 200 kg/h containing 30 wt% acetic acid by countercurrent multistage extraction. The desired exit acid concentration in the aqueous phase is 4 wt%. Find the compositions and amounts of the ether extract V₁ and the aqueous raffinate LN.

5.4 The operating point Δ and stepping stages

Balance around stage 1 and rearrange. The difference between passing streams is again constant everywhere:

Δ = L₀ − V₁ = Ln − Vn+1 = LN − VN+1
xΔ = (L₀x₀ − V₁y₁)/(L₀ − V₁)

Eq. 12.7-10. Geometrically, Δ is the intersection of line L₀V₁ with line LNVN+1 — and it usually falls well outside the triangle.

The stepping recipe

  1. Plot VN+1, L₀, LN; find M; locate V₁ on line LN–M.
  2. Locate Δ = intersection of L₀V₁ and LNVN+1.
  3. From L₀ draw the line to Δ; where it cuts the envelope is V₁ (mass balance).
  4. Draw the tie line through V₁ to get L₁ (equilibrium).
  5. Draw L₁Δ to get V₂. Tie line through V₂ gives L₂.
  6. Alternate balance → equilibrium until you pass LN. Count the tie lines.
Balance lines all pass through Δ. Equilibrium lines are tie lines. Never mix the two up — that is the single most common error in this construction.

Example 12.7-2 · number of stages

450 kg/h pure isopropyl ether extracts 150 kg/h of a 30 wt% acetic acid solution; the exit aqueous phase is to contain 10 wt% acid.

📐 The fan of lines from Δ is almost vertical here, so small differences in how you read the envelope change the stage count by half a stage or more. Geankoplis reads 2.5 stages from this construction. Always sanity-check the answer with an overall mass balance before you trust it.

5.5 Minimum solvent rate

  • Lower the solvent rate and Δ moves. At some point a line through Δ coincides with a tie line.
  • There the driving force vanishes: infinite stages would be needed.
  • That is Vmin — the minimum solvent for the specified separation.
  • Real designs use 1.2–1.5 × Vmin, most often 1.5.
Procedure: extend the line LNVN+1; draw the tie line through L₀ and extend it; their intersection is Δmin. The Δ you actually use must lie closer to VN+1 than Δmin.

The trade-off

More solvent → fewer stages but a more dilute extract and a bigger solvent-recovery still. Less solvent → a rich extract but a tall, expensive column. The 1.5 × Vmin rule of thumb is where those two costs usually balance.

V′ = 1.5 V′min  →  finite N, sensible economics

Immiscible liquids & tower design

When B and C barely dissolve in each other and A is dilute, the triangular diagram disappears. You get a straight operating line, a McCabe–Thiele staircase, and an analytical equation that needs no graph paper at all.

6.1 The operating line

Carrier B and solvent C are treated as constant, so balances are written on the solute-free flows L′ and V′ using ratio units.

L′[x₀/(1−x₀)] + V′[yN+1/(1−yN+1)] = L′[xN/(1−xN)] + V′[y₁/(1−y₁)]

Eq. 12.7-12, overall. Over the first n stages (Eq. 12.7-13) the same form gives the operating line, slope L′/V′:

L′[x₀/(1−x₀)] + V′[yn+1/(1−yn+1)] = L′[xn/(1−xn)] + V′[y₁/(1−y₁)]
  • L′ = kg carrier B/h · V′ = kg solvent C/h — both constant.
  • x = mass fraction A in the feed/raffinate; y = mass fraction A in the solvent/extract.
  • Dilute solutions → the line is essentially straight on an x–y plot.
  • Concentrated ones → it curves, so plot several points.

Stepping off stages

Exactly the McCabe–Thiele staircase you know from distillation and absorption, but between an extraction operating line and the equilibrium curve.

  1. Plot the equilibrium curve y vs x.
  2. Plot the operating line through (x₀, y₁) and (xN, yN+1).
  3. From (x₀, y₁) step horizontally to the equilibrium curve → x₁.
  4. Step vertically to the operating line → y₂.
  5. Repeat until you pass xN. Count the steps.
Real stages are never ideal, so divide by the stage efficiency — and remember partial miscibility of the solvents will add stages in practice.

Extraction equipment

All of it does the same two jobs: disperse one liquid in the other to create interfacial area, then coalesce the phases again so they can be drawn off. Everything else is a variation on how hard you agitate.

7.1 Equipment gallery

1.0 · Stagewise

Mixer–settler

A mechanical mixer disperses one phase into the other as small droplets for fast mass transfer; the settler lets them coalesce again. Droplets must not be too small or settling time blows out. Stage efficiency 75–100% — the benchmark every other contactor is judged against.

Fig. 7.1 — Separate mixer and settler (they can also be combined in one vessel)
2.0 · Differential

Spray column

An empty shell. Either phase may be dispersed: the light phase enters through a distributor at the bottom, rises through the continuous heavy phase and coalesces at the top interface. Simple and cheap, but large back-mixing in the continuous phase kills efficiency — rarely used.

Fig. 7.2 — Spray-type extraction tower, light phase dispersed
3.0 · Stagewise

Perforated-plate / sieve-tray tower

The extraction twin of a distillation sieve tray. Light solvent is redispersed through the perforations at every tray, coalesces under the tray above, while the heavy aqueous phase flows across each plate and down through the downcomer. Repeated redispersion is what beats the spray column.

Fig. 7.3 — Perforated-plate extraction tower
4.0 · Differential

Packed extraction tower

Packing forces droplets to coalesce and redisperse at frequent intervals, raising interfacial area and mass-transfer rate well above a spray column. Still back-mixes, so it is used only when few stages are needed, and it is unsuitable for suspensions or viscous liquids. Check flooding with the Crawford–Wilke correlation.

Fig. 7.4 — Packed extraction tower
5.0 · Mechanically agitated

Scheibel rotating-agitator tower

A series of rotating turbine agitators creates the dispersion; it coalesces on passing through the knitted mesh, then moves through the outer settling zone. The tower behaves as a stack of mixer-settlers and runs at high efficiency.

Fig. 7.5 — Scheibel extraction tower
5.1 · Mechanically agitated

Karr reciprocating-plate tower

Perforated trays are pulsed up and down to agitate the liquid. The shear force is uniform across the cross-section, so the drop-size distribution is uniform — a real advantage over rotary agitators when you scale up.

Fig. 7.6 — Karr reciprocating-plate extraction tower

7.2 Scale-up

Scale-up is done at constant throughput per unit area (VC + VD), constant plate spacing and constant amplitude. Diameter follows the total combined flow rate:

D₂/D₁ = (Q₂/Q₁)0.4
HETS₂/HETS₁ = (D₂/D₁)0.38  or  (D₂/D₁)0.5
SPM₂/SPM₁ = (D₁/D₂)0.14

HETS grows with diameter — a big column is less efficient per metre than the pilot unit, which is exactly why you cannot scale on height alone.

What drying is

The removal of a relatively small amount of water or organic liquid from a process material — usually thermally, usually as the last step before packaging.

1.1 Definition and purpose

  • The common method is thermal: heat the material, evaporate the moisture, carry the vapour away in a gas.
  • The same equipment and calculations apply to removing organic liquids, not just water.
  • Usually the final processing step, so the product must come out handleable and stable.
  • Water can also be removed mechanically — pressing, centrifuging — which is much cheaper than thermal drying.
💡 Design rule: squeeze out mechanically everything you can before you pay to evaporate the rest. Latent heat is expensive; a centrifuge is not.

Example of a specification: dried table salt at 0.5% water.

Methods and dryer types

Two ways to run a dryer, five physical routes to remove the water, and one family tree that organises every machine you will meet.

6.1 Batch or continuous

Batch

Material is loaded into the equipment and drying proceeds for a given period of time. Flexible, easy to clean, good for small campaigns and expensive products.

Continuous

Material is continuously added and dried product continuously removed. Steady state, high throughput, lower cost per tonne.

6.2 Physical methods of drying

Drying is categorised by the physical conditions used to move the water out. The phase diagram shows why the last three are so different.

  • Direct contact with heated air at atmospheric pressure — vapour is removed by the air. Spray drying is the classic example.
  • Vacuum drying — heated indirectly through a metal wall or by radiation, at reduced pressure.
  • Freeze drying — water is sublimed from frozen material.
  • Supercritical drying — the liquid becomes gas with no surface tension and no capillary stress, so the structure survives (gels → aerogels).
  • Infrared / zone drying — radiation penetrates the material and converts to heat inside it, so water leaves more evenly.

Drying equipment

Seven machines you should be able to sketch, label and justify. Each solves the same problem — get hot gas to the moisture and the vapour away — for a different feed form.

7.1 Equipment gallery

Batch · lumpy or pasty

Tray (shelf / cabinet) dryer

Solid is spread uniformly on removable metal trays to a depth of 10–100 mm. Steam-heated air is recirculated by a fan over and parallel to the tray surfaces; electrical heating is used for low loads. Only 10–20% of the air passing the trays is fresh — the rest is recirculated to save energy. After drying, the cabinet is opened and the trays swapped for a fresh batch.

Continuous tunnel dryer

Batch truck or tray compartments operated in series. Solids move continuously through a tunnel while hot gas passes over each tray — countercurrent, concurrent or combined. For granular solids a perforated or screen belt carries a 25–150 mm layer while heated air is blown up through the bed. The dryer is built in sections, each with its own fan and heating coils.

Continuous · granular

Rotary dryer

A hollow cylinder, rotated and inclined toward the outlet. Wet granular solids are fed at the high end and move through the shell as it turns; flights lift them and shower them down through the hot gas. Heating can be direct or indirect through the wall. Typically 0.3–7 m diameter, 1–30 m long, evaporating 5–50 kg water per hour per m³ of dryer volume, with residence times of 5 minutes to 2 hours.

Spray dryer

Liquid or slurry is sprayed into a hot gas stream as a mist of fine droplets, formed by spray nozzles or a high-speed rotating disk. Water vaporises rapidly, leaving dry solid particles. The chamber must be large enough that droplets never touch a wall before they are dry. Solids leave the bottom via a screw conveyor; exhaust gas passes through a cyclone. Gas and liquid flow may be countercurrent, concurrent or combined. Particles come out light, porous and uniformly spherical — think milk powder, detergents and fertiliser pellets.

Drum dryer

A heated metal roll onto which a thin layer of liquid or slurry is applied and evaporated to dryness. The dry solid is scraped off by a knife as the drum revolves slowly. Suited to slurries or pastes of fine suspensions; the drum functions as evaporator and dryer at once. Fluidised bed dryer

An upward air stream fluidises the bed. Turbulent mixing gives excellent heat and mass transfer and a uniform solid temperature, while the solids are treated gently. 7.3 How dryer selection is really done

Drying technologies have grown more diverse and complex, and selection has become correspondingly harder — stricter quality specifications, higher production rates, higher energy costs and tighter environmental regulation all pull in different directions.

Moisture

Nature and location

Is it free or strongly bound to the solid? Sitting near the surface or distributed through the material? These decide whether you are fighting a boundary layer or internal diffusion.

Kinetics

Rate-limiting mechanism

Drying kinetics play a significant role — which mechanism of moisture transfer controls tells you whether more air velocity will help at all.

Product

Physical size and form

Feed presentation is decisive: solid, slurry, paste, flowing powder, filter cake or fibrous material each rule out whole families of dryer.

Medium

Conditions of the drying medium

Temperature, humidity and flow rate of the hot air in a convective dryer — and the pressure in the dryer, kept low for heat-sensitive products.

Quality

Specification may override cost

Demands on product quality may not allow the least expensive option to be chosen on heat and mass transfer grounds alone.

Compliance

GMP and hygiene

Some products require adherence to Good Manufacturing Practice and hygienic equipment design and operation — that constrains the machine before thermodynamics does.

Three practical questions

  • Feed presentation. Solid, slurry, paste, flowing powder, filter cake, fibrous — this comes first.
  • Heating choice. If the material is temperature sensitive, is convective heat applied directly acceptable at all?
  • Agitation. It gives good mixing and so good heat distribution, but it can produce fines — a dust hazard — or damage fragile material. Leave the product stationary instead and you risk hot spots in the solid.

The general choice then falls between shelf/tray dryers, continuous tunnel and rotary dryers, drums, spray dryers and fluidised beds.

⚠️ Non-aqueous solvents

When drying an organic solvent, or a mixture of water and a solvent as in many pharmaceutical products, care is needed to recover the solvent and to avoid the potential danger of fire and explosion. This is usually what forces an indirect or closed-loop dryer.

Changing the operating conditions of the same dryer also changes product quality. The dryer type and the right operating conditions have to be chosen together for optimal quality and cost of thermal dehydration.

Humidity fundamentals

Drying air can only hold so much water. Every calculation in this topic starts from how much it currently holds, and how much more it could.

2.1 Vapour pressure of water

  • Pure water exists as ice, liquid or vapour depending on pressure and temperature.
  • Along line AB, liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium — this is the vapour-pressure line of water.
  • Boiling occurs when the vapour pressure equals the total pressure above the surface.
  • Heating ice from (3) to (4) at low pressure makes it sublime straight to vapour — the basis of freeze drying.
Humidification

Water moves from the liquid phase into the air.

Dehumidification

The reverse — vapour is transferred back to liquid.

2.2 Defining humidity

Humidity, H

Mass of water vapour per unit mass of dry air.

mol vapour / mol dry air = pA / (P − pA)
H = (18.02/28.97) · pA/(P − pA)

MW water 18.02, MW air 28.97, so the factor is 0.622. The slides quote it as 18/29 — same number to three figures.

Saturation humidity, Hs

Hs = (18.02/28.97) · pAS/(P − pAS)

Air in equilibrium with liquid water at that temperature and pressure. pAS is the vapour pressure from the steam tables.

Percentage humidity, Hp

Hp = 100 H / Hs

Actual humidity as a percentage of the saturation humidity at the same temperature and pressure. This is what the lines on the Geankoplis chart show.

Relative humidity, HR

HR = 100 pA / pAS

A ratio of pressures, not of humidities — which is why Hp and HR are close but never equal (78.3% vs 78.9% in Example 9.3-1).

Do not use them interchangeably. Hp uses humidities; HR uses partial pressures. Hp is always the smaller of the two.

2.3 Humid heat and humid volume

Humid heat, cS

The heat in kJ needed to raise 1 kg of dry air plus the vapour it carries by 1 K.

cS = 1.005 + 1.88H   kJ/(kg dry air·K)

From cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K for dry air and 1.88 kJ/kg·K for water vapour, both taken as constant over the working range.

Humid volume, vH

Total volume in m³ of 1 kg of dry air plus its vapour, at 101.325 kPa and temperature T.

vH = (22.41/273)·T·(1/28.97 + H/18.02)
vH = (2.83×10⁻³ + 4.56×10⁻³ H)·T   m³/kg dry air

T in kelvin. Forgetting to convert from °C is the classic slip here.

The psychrometric chart

Five properties, one chart. Know any two and the other three are yours — no steam tables, no iteration.

3.1 Adiabatic saturation temperature

Contact an air–water mixture with a recirculating spray of water in an insulated chamber. The water temperature drifts until it reaches a steady value — the adiabatic saturation temperature TS.

  • A large amount of water contacts the entering gas.
  • Both the temperature and the humidity of the gas change.
  • The gas leaves cooler and wetter, at HS and TS.
(H − HS)/(T − TS) = −cS/λS = −(1.005 + 1.88H)/λS

This is the equation of the adiabatic humidification curves on the chart. Each one passes through HS, TS at 100% saturation. λS is the latent heat of vaporisation at TS.

3.2 Dry bulb and wet bulb

T · dry bulb

The ordinary temperature

What a plain thermometer reads in the air stream. Nothing subtle about it.

Tw · wet bulb

The evaporating temperature

A steady-state non-equilibrium temperature reached when a small amount of water is contacted adiabatically by a continuous gas stream. Because the water is small, the gas temperature and humidity are essentially unchanged.

Evaporation from the wick takes energy, so the bulb cools below the dry-bulb temperature until the heat arriving from the warm air exactly balances the heat needed to evaporate the water.

For air–water, Tw ≈ TS. That coincidence is why you can use one set of curves on the chart for both — and it does not hold for other vapour–gas systems.

In purely convective drying, the drying surface sits at Tw throughout the constant-rate period. That is the fact §5 will build on.

3.3 Reading the chart

  • The chart plots properties of air–water mixtures at 1 atm.
  • The curve marked 100% is the saturation humidity Hs against temperature.
  • Any point below the saturation line is an unsaturated mixture.
  • Curves below it are lines of constant percentage humidity Hp, in 10% steps.
  • Going vertically down from the saturation line at a fixed temperature, the gap to H = 0 is divided evenly into ten 10% increments.
Five properties live on the chart: dry bulb, wet bulb, dew point, humidity and relative (or percentage) humidity. Fix any two and the other three follow.

The three constructions you need

  1. Dew point: move horizontally left at constant H to the saturation curve. That temperature is where the mixture would first condense.
  2. Wet bulb: follow the adiabatic saturation line up-left to the saturation curve.
  3. Percentage humidity: read the labelled curve through the point, or divide H by Hs at the same dry-bulb temperature.

Moisture in solids

Not all the water in a solid can be removed, and not all of it behaves the same way. Four definitions decide how far a dryer can actually go.

4.1 Equilibrium moisture content, X*

  • Moisture content is expressed as water as a percentage of the dry material — a dry basis, kg water per kg moisture-free solid.
  • Leave a solid in air of fixed humidity long enough and it settles at its equilibrium moisture content X*. No dryer using that air can go below it.
  • X* depends on the structure of the solid, the gas temperature and the gas moisture content.
  • It varies enormously between materials at the same relative humidity — leather and wool hold far more than soap or glass wool.
  • It decreases as temperature rises, and is taken as constant over moderate temperature ranges.

Typical equilibrium moisture contents at about 298 K. Curve shapes are illustrative — read real values from the chart in the notes.

4.2 Four kinds of moisture

Bound moisture

Water retained so that it exerts a vapour pressure less than that of free water at the same temperature. Held in small capillaries, adsorbed on surfaces, or in solution in cell walls. Hard to remove.

Unbound moisture

Moisture that exerts a vapour pressure equal to that of pure liquid at the same temperature. Behaves like a puddle sitting on the solid.

Free moisture

Water in excess of the equilibrium moisture content: X − X*. This is the only part a dryer can actually remove, and it is what drying-rate curves are plotted against.

Humid heat

The heat needed to raise unit mass of dry air and its associated vapour through one degree — J/(kg·K) or Btu/(lb·°F). Same cS as §2.3.

🔑 Free moisture X − X* is the driving quantity. A solid at X = 0.25 with X* = 0.05 has only 0.20 kg/kg available to remove, however long you run the dryer.

4.3 Wet basis vs dry basis

Wet basis

Fraction of the wet mass

200 kg of moist solids at 30% moisture, wet basis:

water = 0.30 × 200 = 60 kg
dry solid = 0.70 × 200 = 140 kg

Straight percentages of the total. This is how industry quotes moisture.

Dry basis

Fraction of the dry mass

200 kg of moist solids at 30% moisture, dry basis. Take 100 kg of solid and 30 kg of water, so 130 kg total, then scale:

water = (30/130) × 200 = 46.2 kg
dry solid = (100/130) × 200 = 153.8 kg

Same headline number, 14 kg less water. This is the basis every drying equation uses.

Rate of drying

One experiment, two plots, two periods. The first half of the water leaves at a flat predictable rate; the last fraction takes three times as long. Everything here is about knowing where that switch happens and designing around it.

5.1 The experiment and the rate

What must match the plant

  • Ratio of drying to non-drying surface
  • Bed depth
  • Air velocity, humidity and temperature
  • Direction of air flow relative to the surface

Take the slope of the tangent to the X vs t curve at several times:

R = − (LS/A) · dX/dt

R is the water flux in kg H₂O/h·m², LS the kg of dry solid, A the exposed drying area. X is the free moisture Xt − X* from §4 — never the total.

The minus sign earns its place: X falls with time, so dX/dt is negative and R comes out positive.

5.2 Walk the curve

Click a segment. The left plot is what the balance records; the right plot is the same run replotted as rate against free moisture.

The flat height of the plateau, in kg H₂O/h·m². For this curve, 1.51.

Where the plateau breaks and the surface can no longer stay wet. Here 0.195 kg H₂O/kg dry solid.

5.3 The constant-rate period

surface

A continuous film

A film of water covers the drying surface, and all of it is unbound water.

rate

Independent of the solid

Evaporation matches a free liquid surface at the same air conditions. Surface roughness can raise it a little.

supply

Fed from inside

In a porous solid the water comes from the interior. The period lasts only while the surface is resupplied as fast as it evaporates.

Rate-limiting step: heat and mass transfer through the boundary layer, not anything inside the solid. At steady state the mass transfer rate exactly balances the heat transfer rate — which is why the surface sits at the wet-bulb temperature you learned to read in §3.

5.5 Effect of process variables

Air velocity

Rc ∝ G0.8

With no conduction or radiation, Rc follows h, and h follows G0.8 for parallel flow. Doubling the velocity raises the rate by about 74%. The effect matters less once radiation and conduction are present.

Gas humidity

Lower H → higher Rc

Drop H at fixed T and TW falls with it, so the driving force (T − TW) in Eq. 9.6-7 widens and the rate rises.

Gas temperature

Higher T → higher Rc

Raising T also raises TW, but by less — the difference grows, so the rate goes up.

Solid thickness

Rc unchanged, t ∝ x

For convection only the thickness does not affect the rate — but the time from X₁ to X₂ is directly proportional to it, since a thicker bed holds more water per m².

R = RcX/Xc  9.7-9

5.8 Convection + radiation + conduction

Everything so far assumed convection only. In a real tray dryer the solid also sees radiation from hot surfaces and conduction through the metal tray — and the surface no longer sits at the wet-bulb temperature.

Heat arrives three ways; water leaves one way. Conduction reaches the solid by convection from gas to the metal plate, then conduction through the metal and finally through the solid itself.

Freeze drying

Lyophilization. Freeze the water, drop the pressure below the triple point, and let the ice leave as vapour without ever becoming liquid. No liquid means no capillary collapse — which is why the structure survives.

8.1 Why freeze dry at all

  • An alternative for substances that are damaged by moderate temperature in ordinary drying.
  • Produces the highest quality food products: sublimation gives structural rigidity to the frozen sample, so the porous structure left behind does not collapse.
  • Runs at low pressure or high vacuum, because the vapour pressure of ice is very small.
  • For pure water, sublimation happens at or near 0 °C and 4.58 mm Hg absolute.
  • If the water is in solution or combined, the material must be cooled below 0 °C to keep it solid — most freeze drying runs at about −10 °C, or at lower pressure around 2000 μm.

Advantage

Little loss of flavour and aroma; degradation is minimised. The product rehydrates to something close to its original form.

Disadvantage

Expensive. The drying rate is slow and a vacuum system has to be bought, run and maintained.

Freeze drying goes under the triple point; supercritical drying goes around the critical point. Both avoid a liquid–vapour interface, and so both avoid surface tension pulling the structure in on itself.

8.2 The process, and the retreating ice front

01 · Prepare

The product is made up as it will finally be consumed.

02 · Freeze

Frozen by exposure to very cold air, T < −20 to −50 °C, at P < 610 Pa.

03 · Dry

Water is removed as vapour by sublimation in a vacuum chamber; the vapour is pulled off by a mechanical vacuum pump or steam-jet ejector.

04 · Rehydrate

Add water later and the product recovers much of its original structure.

Fig. 9.11-2

Uniformly retreating ice front

The plane of sublimation starts at the outside surface and recedes inward, leaving a porous shell of already-dried material behind it. Heat must be conducted in through that shell while vapour diffuses out through it — the two fluxes share the same growing resistance.

Heat and mass transfer together

  • The latent heat of sublimation, 2838 kJ/kg of ice, is conducted inward through the dried layer — and sometimes through the frozen layer from the rear.
  • The vaporised water is transferred outward through that same dried layer, so heat and mass transfer occur simultaneously.
  • Heat reaches the dried surface by conduction, convection and/or radiation from the gas phase, then by conduction to the ice layer.
Two constraints on the schedule: total drying time must be long enough to bring the final moisture below about 5 wt% so the product does not degrade in storage; and the maximum temperature reached in both the dried and frozen material must stay low enough to keep degradation minimal.

8.3 The Sandall model

Heat flux to the surface arrives by convection, then passes through the dry solid by conduction to the sublimation surface. Assuming pseudo-steady state, the two are equal:

q = h(Te − Ts) = (k/ΔL)(Ts − Tf)  9.11-1

Mass flux of water vapour away from the sublimation front, through the dried layer and then into the bulk gas:

NA = [D′/(RT·ΔL)](pfw − psw) = kg(psw − pew)  9.11-2

Eliminating the surface values gives two resistances in series on each side:

q = (Te − Tf)/(1/h + ΔL/k)  9.11-3
NA = (pfw − pew)/(1/kg + RT·ΔL/D′)  9.11-4
q = ΔHsNA  9.11-5  ·  pfw = f(Tf)  9.11-6

Substituting 9.11-3, 9.11-4 into 9.11-5 gives the equation that fixes the ice-front temperature:

(Te − Tf)/(1/h + ΔL/k) = ΔHs(pfw − pew)/(1/kg + RT·ΔL/D′)  9.11-7
(Ts − Tf)/(ΔL/k) = ΔHs(pfw − pew)/(1/kg + RT·ΔL/D′)  9.11-8
  • h and kg are set by the gas velocities and the characteristics of the dryer, and are constant.
  • Te and pew are set by the external operating conditions.
  • k and D′ are determined by the nature of the dried material.

Two ceilings on the rate

Te, and therefore Ts, can be raised to speed drying up — but only so far:

  • Ts cannot go too high, or the dried outer material is thermally damaged.
  • Tf must stay well below the melting point, or the frozen core melts and the whole advantage is lost.
When k/ΔL is small compared with kg and D′/RTΔL, the outer surface temperature limit is reached first as Ts is raised — the process is then heat-transfer controlled.

8.4 Drying time

Let x be the fraction of the original water still frozen. The dried layer thickness follows:

ΔL = (1 − x)(L/2)  9.11-9
NA = (L/2)(1/MAVS)(−dx/dt)  9.11-10

where MA is the molecular weight of water, VS the volume of solid occupied by a unit kg of water initially (VS = 1/X₀ρs), X₀ the initial free moisture content and ρs the dry solid bulk density.

Combining 9.11-3, 9.11-5, 9.11-9 and 9.11-10:

(L/2)(ΔHs/MAVS)(−dx/dt) = (Te − Tf)/[1/h + (1 − x)L/2k]  9.11-11

Integrating from x = 1 at t = 0 to x = x₂ at t, for the case where h is large:

t = [L²/(4kVS)](ΔHs/MA)[1/(Te − Tf)](x₁ − x₂ − x₁²/2 + x₂²/2)  9.11-13
Set x₂ = 0 and the slab is completely dry. Note ΔHs/MA is the heat of sublimation expressed per kg of water — 2838 kJ/kg.

8.5 How far to trust the model

Works

65–90% removal

The Sandall model predicts the time well over the removal of 65 to 90% of the total initial water.

Breaks down

The last 10–35%

For the final 10 to 35% the drying rate slowed and the actual time was greater than predicted. Plan for it.

Properties drift

k and D′

Effective k varies significantly with total pressure, the gas present and the material. D′ depends on structure, Knudsen diffusivity and molecular diffusivity.

Also from 9.11-8: when h is large, Te ≈ Ts, and Tf can be solved because Tf and pfw are related by the equilibrium vapour pressure relation, Eq. 9.11-6. Evaluate properties at the mean, T = (Tf + Ts)/2.

Humidification & cooling towers

Bring warm water into direct contact with unsaturated air and some of it evaporates. The latent heat comes out of the water that stays behind — so the water cools itself by giving a little of itself away.

9.1 Humidification

  • Humidification is a process in which a relatively warm liquid is brought into direct contact with unsaturated gas, and some of the liquid is vaporized.
  • The liquid temperature drops because of the latent heat of evaporation.
  • The unit is, in effect, a gas–liquid contactor and separator.
  • Dehumidification is the reverse: water vapour is transferred from the vapour state back into the liquid.
Why it exists at all: in real plants you cannot dump hot water into lakes, and buying fresh cooling water is expensive. Warm water discharged from heat exchangers and condensers is cooled in a tower and recirculated.

Where you meet it

  • Humidifying air for moisture control and air conditioning
  • Dehumidifying air
  • Cooling towers in power plants, oil refineries, petrochemical plants and natural gas plants
warm water + unsaturated air → cooler water + humid air

9.2 The water cooling tower

Counterflow tower

How it is built and how it runs

Slats of plastic or a packed bed form the fill. Warm water enters the top, is distributed through troughs and overflows to cascade over the slat gratings, giving a large interfacial area as droplets and films. Air enters at the bottom and flows upward through the descending water. The upward flow is induced either by the buoyancy of the warm air in the tower — natural draft — or by a fan.

Four things to remember

  • Water cannot be cooled below the wet-bulb temperature of the entering air. In practice you can only approach to about 3 K above it.
  • Humidification and dehumidification both need intimate contact between gas and liquid for large mass and heat transfer.
  • Gas-phase resistance controls the rate of transfer — which is why the design equations are written on the gas side.
  • Spray and packed towers give large interfacial area and promote turbulence in the gas phase.
The natural-draft hyperbolic shell you see at power stations is the same idea in cross-flow: air enters through louvres around the base, passes horizontally through an annular fill area, and the shell's height creates the draft. The hot-water distribution basin sits above the fill; the cold-water collecting basin sits below it.

9.3 What happens at the interface

Fig. 10.5-1

Profiles in the upper part of a cooling tower

Temperature falls from TL in the bulk liquid to Ti at the interface and on to TG in the bulk gas. Humidity falls from the saturated value Hi at the interface to HG in the bulk gas.

  • Water vapour diffuses from the interface to the bulk gas with a driving force in the gas phase of (Hi − HG) kg H₂O/kg dry air.
  • There is no mass transfer in the liquid phase, because the water is pure.
  • Temperature driving force is TL − Ti in the liquid and Ti − TG in the gas.
  • Sensible heat flows from bulk liquid to interface, and from interface into the gas.
  • Latent heat leaves the interface in the water vapour that diffuses into the gas.
The energy balance at the interface: sensible heat flow from the liquid = sensible heat flow in the gas + latent heat flow in the gas. That single statement is what produces Eq. 10.5-14 and the tie line on the enthalpy diagram.

9.5 The design procedure

  1. Plot the saturated-air enthalpy Hyi against Ti, calculated from Eq. 9.3-8 using the saturation humidity, with 0 °C as base. Tabulated in Table 10.5-1.
  2. From the entering air conditions TG1 and H₁, calculate Hy1. Plot (TL1, Hy1) — the desired leaving water temperature — as one point on the operating line, then draw the line with slope LcL/G to end at TL2, the entering water temperature. That fixes Hy2.

Two regimes

Below the boiling point only the surface evaporates, and the rate is set by the difference between the liquid's vapour pressure and the vapour already in the gas. Once psat reaches the pressure above, the whole bulk boils and the rate is set instead by how fast you supply heat.

Every kilogram that leaves takes its latent heat with it. Supply no heat and the liquid cools itself — which is the wet-bulb effect from the drying topic, and the whole basis of a cooling tower.

Drop the pressure and the boiling point falls with it. Try the vacuum preset: the same liquid boils at a far lower temperature, which protects heat-sensitive product and widens ΔT against the steam.

Methods of operation

One effect, or several in series. The whole reason for several is that the vapour boiled off in one effect is still hot enough to be the steam for the next.

2.1 Single effect

One vessel, one pressure, one boiling temperature. Steam condenses on one side of the surface and the solution boils on the other.

  • Simple, low capital cost, easy to control.
  • Steam economy is always below 1 — you never get a full kilogram of vapour for a kilogram of steam, because some heat goes into raising the feed to its boiling point.
  • Fine when steam is cheap, the duty is small, or the product is awkward.

Multiple effect, in one sentence

Put a second vessel at a lower pressure so its boiling temperature is below the first effect's vapour temperature, and that vapour can condense in it as heating steam. Repeat. Roughly 3 kg of water per kg of steam in a triple effect — bought at the expense of the capital cost of the extra vessels.

TS > T₁ > T₂ > T₃  ·  P₁ > P₂ > P₃

2.2 Forward feed

  • Fresh feed enters the first effect and flows on in the same direction as the vapour.
  • The first effect runs hot enough that its vapour heats the second; the second's vapour heats the third.
  • If the feed enters near its boiling point, 1 kg of steam evaporates almost 1 kg in each effect in turn.
  • Boiling temperature falls from effect to effect, so if effect 1 is at 1 atm the last is under vacuum.

When to use it

When the feed is hot, or when the final concentrated product would be damaged at high temperature — the most concentrated liquor ends up in the coolest effect. The catch: the liquor gets more viscous as it moves to lower temperatures, so U falls along the train.

2.3 Backward and parallel feed

Backward feed

Feed enters the last and coldest effect and flows counter to the vapour, so the most concentrated liquor sits in the hottest effect. That keeps its viscosity down and U up — good for cold feed and viscous products, bad for anything heat sensitive. Needs pumps between effects, because liquid must move from low to high pressure.

Parallel feed

Fresh feed is added to every effect and product is drawn from each. No liquid flows between effects. Used mainly in crystallising evaporators, where the product is a slurry of crystals that you do not want to pump from vessel to vessel.

Types of evaporator

Eight machines. Each one exists because the one before it could not handle something — viscosity, heat sensitivity, scale, or cost.

3.1 The gallery

1 · Batch or continuous

Open kettle or pan

The simplest evaporator — an open pan in which the liquid boils, heated by steam condensing in a jacket or in coils immersed in the liquid, sometimes direct-fired. Inexpensive and easy to operate, but poor heat economy. Paddles or scrapers provide agitation.

2 · Natural circulation

Horizontal-tube evaporator

Steam enters the tubes, condenses, and leaves at the other end; the boiling solution sits outside and covers them. Vapour leaves the liquid surface and passes a de-entraining baffle to stop liquid droplets being carried over. Cheap, good for non-viscous liquids with a high heat-transfer coefficient that do not deposit scale — but liquid circulation is poor.

3 · Natural circulation

Vertical short-tube evaporator

Liquid inside vertical tubes, steam condensing outside. Boiling lowers the density so the liquid rises by natural circulation, raising the heat-transfer coefficient, then flows back down through a large central open space — the downcomer. Not suitable for viscous liquids. The basket type is a variation.

4 · Once-through

Long-tube vertical evaporator

Liquid passes once through the tubes and is not circulated. Vapour bubbles forming inside create a pumping action that gives high liquid velocities. Tubes are 3 to 10 m long and the contact time is low — which is why it suits condensed milk.

5 · Thin film

Falling-film evaporator

Liquid is fed to the top of the tubes and flows down the walls as a thin film; vapour–liquid separation happens at the bottom. Hold-up time is very small and heat-transfer coefficients are high, so it is the standard choice for heat-sensitive materials such as fruit juices.

6 · Pumped

Forced-circulation evaporator

A pump forces circulation through the tubes, raising the liquid-film heat-transfer coefficient. Vertical tubes are short. Useful for viscous liquids; alternatively the heat exchanger is placed outside, horizontally, as a separate unit.

7 · Mechanically agitated

Agitated-film evaporator

The main resistance is on the liquid side, so mechanical agitation is used to raise turbulence in the film. A single large jacketed tube holds an internal agitator; liquid enters at the top and is spread into a turbulent film by vertical blades. Highest coefficients of all — rubber latex, gelatin, antibiotics, fruit juices — but high cost and small capacity.

8 · Solar

Open-pan solar evaporator

A very old process. Saltwater is put in shallow open pans or troughs and allowed to evaporate slowly in the sun, crystallising the salt. No steam, no economy calculation — just area and time.

4.2 Heat balance

Heat in feed + heat in steam = heat in liquor + heat in vapour + heat in condensate, with no losses:

FhF + SHS = LhL + VHV + ShS  Eq 3

The steam gives up only its latent heat, λ = HS − hS, so:

FhF + Sλ = LhL + VHV  Eq 4
q = UAΔT = UA(TS − T₁) = S(HS − hS) = Sλ
Vapour V is in equilibrium with liquid L, so both leave at T₁. The condensate leaves at TS. λ comes from the steam tables at TS.

Where hF and hL come from

Dilute — sugar, milk, juice, biological solids

Heat of solution ≈ 0, so use sensible heat with a datum:

hF = cpF(TF − Tdatum) · hL = cpL(TL − Tdatum)

cpF and cpL are often just the cp of water.

Concentrated — NaOH, H₂SO₄, CaCl₂

Heat of solution is not negligible. Read hF and hL from an enthalpy–concentration chart, which exists for only a few systems.

Datum trick: take the datum at T₁. Then hL = 0, HV = λ at T₁, and Eq. 4 collapses to SλS = Vλ₁ − Fcp(TF − T₁).

◆ Learning outcome 3

Processing variables

Pressure, boiling-point rise, the overall coefficient, and what fouling does to all three.

5.1 Pressure and steam economy

steam economy = V/S = kg vaporised / kg steam used
  • Lowering the pressure in the vapour space lowers T₁, which widens ΔT = TS − T₁ and so raises capacity for the same area.
  • It also lets heat-sensitive products boil cool — the reason the last effect usually runs under vacuum.
  • Raising the steam pressure raises TS and ΔT too, but costs more and risks damaging the product.
  • A single effect can never reach an economy of 1; a triple effect approaches 3.

Corrosion, scale and materials

Corrosion and scale reduce the overall heat-transfer coefficient and the efficiency. Materials for the evaporator should be chosen to minimise corrosion and contamination of the product — which in food and pharmaceutical duty also means surfaces that can be cleaned, because anything left behind grows bacteria.

Foaming, entrainment of droplets in the vapour, and product degradation at the tube wall are the other three things that quietly wreck an evaporator's performance.

5.2 Boiling-point rise and Dühring's rule

A dissolved solute raises the boiling point above that of pure water at the same pressure. The empirical Dühring rule says the boiling point of a solution of given concentration is a straight-line function of the boiling point of pure water at the same pressure.

BPR = Tboiling, solution − Tboiling, water  (at the same pressure)
  • BPR eats into your driving force: ΔT = TS − T₁ and T₁ = Tsat + BPR.
  • Common salts give large BPR; biological solutes — sucrose, citric acid, glycerol — give quite small values.
  • For sugar the deck gives BPR (°C) = 1.78x + 6.22x², with x the mass fraction.
Example 8.4-2. At 25.6 kPa water boils at 65.6 °C. From the Dühring chart, 30% NaOH boils at 79.5 °C, so BPR = 13.9 °C.

5.3 Enthalpy–concentration charts and U

Enthalpy–concentration charts

Plot enthalpy of solution against concentration in wt%, with temperature as a parameter. Needed whenever the heat of solution is not negligible — NaOH is the classic case. They exist for only a few systems, which is why most problems are set on dilute solutions where cp will do.

Overall coefficient U

Combines the condensing-steam film, the tube wall, any scale, and the boiling-liquid film. The liquid side usually controls, which is the whole justification for forced circulation and agitated-film designs. U falls as the liquor thickens — so it falls along a forward-feed train and rises along a backward-feed one.

Multiple-effect calculations

Each effect is a single effect. What links them is that the vapour from one is the steam for the next — and that the available ΔT has to be shared out.

6.1 How the effects link

  • Raw steam heats effect 1, boiling at T₁ and P₁.
  • Vapour from effect 1 condenses in effect 2, vaporising water at T₂ and P₂.
  • For heat to flow, T₂ must be below the condensing temperature, so P₂ < P₁. Same again for effect 3.
  • At steady state, flow rates, rates of evaporation, pressures and temperatures are all constant. To change the final concentration you change the feed rate to the first effect.
ΣΔT = ΔT₁ + ΔT₂ + ΔT₃ = TS − T₃ − ΣBPR

If every effect has the same area and the same heat load q:

q = U₁AΔT₁ = U₂AΔT₂ = U₃AΔT₃

so the temperature differences are inversely proportional to the overall coefficients:

ΔT₁ = ΣΔT · (1/U₁)/(1/U₁ + 1/U₂ + 1/U₃)
That is the first-trial estimate. The low-U effect gets the biggest share of the driving force.

6.2 The step-by-step method

  1. Find the boiling point in the last effect from its known pressure and outlet concentration. Add BPR from the Dühring plot if there is one.
  2. Get the total vapour from an overall mass balance. Assume equal evaporation in each effect (V₁ = V₂ = V₃) and find L₁, L₂, L₃, then the solid concentration in each from solute balances.
  3. Estimate ΔT₁, ΔT₂, ΔT₃ from the 1/U rule above.
  4. Use heat and mass balances on each effect to calculate the amount vaporised and the liquid flows.
  5. Calculate q for each effect and the area; take Am, the average.
  6. Start trial 2 with the new L and V values; get new ΔT values by scaling on the areas.
  7. Repeat steps 4–5 until the areas from each effect are reasonably close.

Evaporation of biological materials

Food and pharmaceutical liquors degrade when they are held hot. Every design decision here is about shortening the time at temperature.

What makes them different

  • Heat sensitive — colour, flavour, vitamins and enzymes are lost quickly at high temperature.
  • So evaporate under vacuum, at a low boiling point, with the shortest possible hold-up time.
  • Many become viscous as they concentrate, so the liquid-film coefficient collapses just when you need it most.
  • Some foam, and most will foul.
  • Equipment must be easy to clean — residues support bacterial growth.
  • Biological solutes have small BPR values compared with common salts, so the driving force is not eaten away.

A separation technique in which solid particles form from a homogeneous liquid solution — and, uniquely among the separations in this course, one where you are designing the shape and size of the product, not just its purity.

Solubility and supersaturation

The solubility curve is the map. Everything above it is where crystallization happens — but only part of that region is safe to operate in.

2.1 The three regions

solubility curvemetastable limityour operating point
60 °C
45
  • Undersaturated — below the curve. Crystals dissolve; nothing forms.
  • Metastable — just above it. Existing crystals grow, but no new nuclei appear spontaneously. This is where industry operates.
  • Labile — far above it. Spontaneous nucleation floods the vessel with tiny crystals.
Cool along a path and you move left on this plot at constant concentration; evaporate and you move up. Most industrial crystallizers do a bit of both, and the art is staying inside the metastable band the whole way.

2.2 Why small crystals dissolve while big ones grow

  • The solubility of a very small crystal — below about a micrometre — is greater than that of a larger crystal.
  • This is due to the high surface energy per unit mass of small particles.
  • So in a supersaturated solution a small crystal can sit in equilibrium, or even dissolve, while larger crystals with their lower solubility keep growing by consuming solute from the solution.
The effect of particle size is therefore very important in nucleation: a cluster must reach a critical size before it is stable enough to survive and grow. Below that, it redissolves.

Ostwald ripening, in one line

Leave a suspension long enough and the small crystals disappear into the large ones, because the solution is simultaneously supersaturated with respect to the big crystals and undersaturated with respect to the small ones. Your size distribution keeps drifting even after supersaturation is exhausted.

Material balance and yield

At the end of the process the solution has reached solubility equilibrium — it is saturated at the final temperature. That one fact closes every yield calculation in this topic.

3.1 The method

  1. Split the feed into solute and water.
  2. Subtract any water evaporated, W.
  3. Write a balance on water and a balance on the solute.
  4. Close them with the solubility at the final temperature — read from the solubility curve.
  5. Solve the two equations simultaneously for C and S.
Hydrates change everything. If the crystal is Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, each kilogram of crystal carries away 180.2/286.2 = 0.63 kg of water as well as the salt. Forget that and your water balance is wrong by more than half.
solute in feed = solute in crystals + solute in mother liquor
water in feed = W + water of hydration in crystals + water in mother liquor
solute fraction in crystal = MWanhydrous / MWhydrate

Section 04

Nucleation

The formation of a tiny, stable solid nucleus from the liquid phase. Get this wrong and you make a vessel full of dust that will not filter.

4.1 Homogeneous nucleation

  • Molecules in a supersaturated solution collide and form clusters.
  • Most clusters redissolve — they are below the critical size, where surface energy still outweighs the energy gained from forming the lattice.
  • A cluster that reaches critical size becomes a stable nucleus and can grow.
  • It needs high supersaturation, which is why it is rare in a well-run industrial crystallizer.
Homogeneous nucleation happens in the bulk with nothing to help it. In practice, dust, vessel walls and existing crystals almost always get there first.

Contact nucleation — the industrial reality

New crystals are produced by contact: crystal against impeller, crystal against vessel wall, crystal against crystal. Interference from agitator blades is the dominant source in a stirred crystallizer.

  • It happens at low supersaturation, where homogeneous nucleation cannot.
  • Its rate depends on agitation intensity, so it is something you can actually control.
  • It is the mechanism the MSMPR model in §6 is really describing.
Commercial practice: operate at low supersaturation with agitation to produce more uniform crystals. Low supersaturation suppresses the spontaneous burst; controlled agitation supplies a steady, predictable trickle of new nuclei instead.

Section 05
◆ Learning outcome 4

Crystal growth

The growth of those nuclei that reached critical size. Nucleation and growth run simultaneously for as long as supersaturation exists — and whichever dominates decides your product.

5.1 Principles

  • Growth is the subsequent growth of nuclei that succeed in achieving the critical cluster size.
  • Nucleation and growth continue simultaneously while supersaturation exists.
  • Depending on conditions, either may predominate — so crystals of different sizes and shapes are obtained. Controlling size and shape is one of the main challenges in industrial manufacture, particularly for pharmaceuticals.
  • Once supersaturation is exhausted the system reaches equilibrium and crystallization is complete — unless conditions are changed to supersaturate the solution again.

Rate of crystal growth

Defined as the distance moved per unit time in a direction perpendicular to the face.

  • A layer-by-layer process: solute travels from the bulk solution to the outer crystal face by diffusion, then integrates into the lattice.
  • A mass transfer coefficient applies to the diffusion step; a further resistance occurs at the face when molecules integrate into the lattice.
  • The solution must be supersaturated for both steps to proceed.
The ΔL law: geometrically similar crystals of the same material suspended in the same solution grow at the same rate, regardless of their size. That is what makes G a single number for the whole population — and it is the assumption behind §6.

5.2 Two resistances in series

MSMPR crystallizer design

Mixed-suspension, mixed-product-removal. One model that ties nucleation rate, growth rate and material balance together — and turns a screen analysis into two design numbers.

6.1 Population density

  • Plot the cumulative number of crystals N per litre of suspension of size L and smaller, against L.
  • The slope dN/dL of that line is the crystal population density n, in number of crystals per litre per mm.
  • For a screen cut, Lav = (L₁ + L₂)/2, where L₁ is the opening of the upper screen.
n = dN/dL = mcut / (α ρ Lav³ ΔL)

mcut is the mass of crystals in that cut per litre, α the shape factor and ρ the crystal density.

A plot of ln n against L is a straight line, with intercept ln n⁰ and slope −1/(Gτ). Two numbers off one graph: the growth rate and the nuclei population density.
ln n = ln n⁰ − L/(Gτ)
G = −1/(slope × τ)
B⁰ = n⁰G  nuclei/h·L
La = 3.67 Gτ  dominant size
1 − Wf = e−z(1 + z + z²/2 + z³/6),   z = L/(Gτ)

The last one predicts the cumulative weight fraction at any screen opening, which is how you check the model against the sieve data you started from.

Coefficient of variation, CV, characterises the spread of the size distribution. For product removed from a mixed-suspension crystallizer, CV is about 50% — a wide distribution is inherent to the design, not a fault.
